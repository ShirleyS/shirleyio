The honest version

First, the obvious: in 2023 I stepped away from tech roles, not from the work. Since then my partner and I have built five businesses in Mexico City from zero, and I built the platform that runs them. The last two years went into engineering myself out of the day-to-day: hiring and developing the team, writing the processes, and shipping RIOS so nothing depends on me. It worked. My partner leads the full operation now with a capable team behind her, and the businesses run whether I’m in the room or not. With the businesses running on their own, the timing is right to go back in.

The skills never went stale. Running businesses on top of POS, accounting, payroll, and banking systems is the same integration and data work I did for customers like Stripe and Slack, with my own P&L on the line. And I’ve been shipping the whole time: four production systems built with Claude, used every day by operators, homebuyers, founders, and event hosts. Being the customer sharpened exactly what a Solutions Architect needs: what breaks in production, what a buyer actually trusts, and what it takes for a tool to get adopted instead of shelved.

What I’m looking for: a role that builds on work I’ve led for a decade. Pre-sales Solutions Architecture and post-sales Customer Success Engineering for growth-stage and enterprise customers, founder-level technical conversations, and the advisory work that lives between technical evaluation and production deployment. Ideally close to AI: I use Claude daily as an operator and a builder, the capability gains release over release are hard to miss, and I’d rather help customers put that into production than watch it from the outside.

My criteria for any role haven’t changed in ten years, in this order: an overwhelming amount to keep learning, a smart team, and cool products. That framework has never steered me wrong, and it’s the bar for whatever comes next.

RIOSIn production · Public SaaSholarios.com↗

A complete operating system for food businesses, built with an operator's instincts and a product manager's discipline

Sales, purchasing, invoices, bank, payroll, scheduling, inventory, recipes, and P&L in one place. Underneath, RIOS collects and cross-checks data points from every source to detect where money is quietly leaking, and a completeness layer refuses to show a number it can't stand behind. It started as the internal system for our five businesses and now runs as a public product with a free plan and a paid savings flywheel.

The problem

Each business in the portfolio ran on its own mix of POS, inventory, accounting, and staffing tools. Reports disagreed by 5–15% on any given day, and losses hid in the gaps between them: a short cash drawer here, a supplier price creep there. The decisions that mattered (staffing tomorrow, ordering next week, whether last week’s price change worked) were being made on lagged, incomplete, or inconsistent data.

Architecture
  1. 01
    Ingestion

    Adapters for POS, CFDI invoices, bank statements, and PDF/CSV imports backfill history on a rolling cursor and catch late-arriving rows. Every run is logged and idempotent: running the same job twice never duplicates data.

  2. 02
    Completeness

    A completeness layer per company, brand, and location. Every view declares the data it depends on and refuses to render a misleading number when something is missing or stale.

  3. 03
    Reconciliation

    POS sales, terminal payouts, CFDI invoices, and bank deposits compared in the same units, so the numbers actually line up. When they don't, the gap gets flagged instead of averaged away.

  4. 04
    Loss detection

    Independent signals run across POS tickets, voids, discounts, cash counts, invoice lines, recipes, and payroll: short cash drawers, discounts concentrated on one cashier, voided orders that were likely already made, paying a supplier more than your own best price, and ingredient increases eroding plate margin. Every loss is measured in pesos with the receipts attached, opens a tracked episode with an owner, and recovery is re-measured the following month.

  5. 05
    Savings flywheel

    Signal, owned action, pesos recovered and verified against real invoices and bank movements. Purchase lines from every business on RIOS also feed anonymized supplier-price benchmarks, shown only once at least five distinct buyers contribute. Each operator who joins makes the alerts sharper for everyone: a network effect on top of the loop.

  6. 06
    Executive layer

    A small, opinionated set of views (daily sales against target, ticket size, item mix, labor against forecast, the losses open this fortnight) built around what an operator actually decides on, not what's easy to chart.

  7. 07
    Agent layer

    Claude on top, scoped to an explicit memory of business structure, vendors, and known anomalies. Used for explanation, summarization, and drafting weekly operating notes that are reviewed before they leave the system.

AI in the loop

Used. Claude handles the unstructured inputs that feed the database (receipts and statements), extracting structured fields so the deterministic side can reconcile against them. Every output is validated before it lands.

Operational impact
  • Month-end close happens continuously instead of after a three-week wait.
  • Waste down 5% in the first month, driven by real-time margin and cost alerting.
  • Losses caught from multiple independent data points (cash, discounts, voids, supplier prices, recipe margins), each with receipts and a measured recovery.
  • AI extracts invoices (CFDIs, PDFs, XMLs) and matches them to bank deposits and POS sales. Manual reconciliation is gone: for us, two full-time jobs.
  • Labor cost as a percent of revenue tracked continuously per location, not at the end of the period.
  • Multi-entity, multi-brand, multi-location P&L in one engine. A new business is online in days.
  • Productized at holarios.com: a free plan with no credit card, and a paid savings flywheel that compounds as more operators join.
